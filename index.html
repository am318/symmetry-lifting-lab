What you are looking at

Each system is a trained network fθ on the plane. A symmetry group G of rotations acts on the inputs, and on the outputs when they are vectors. The Reynolds average PGf averages f over G: a 64-point quadrature for SO(2), an exact average over the n rotations for Cn. The defect is δ = f − PGf.

The orbit direction η = dΠ(ξ)f rotates the function infinitesimally. It is orthogonal to δ, so moving along it changes where the function points, not how symmetric it is.

The two directions

c* = J+η and b* = J+(−δ). J is the Jacobian of the outputs on a polar probe with respect to the parameters. J+ is truncated at σk ≥ 10−3σ1, and the minimum norm is taken in the sensitivity-normalised gauge (or plain ℓ2, by the switch). The lift residual is the part of the target the parameters cannot reach locally.

Resolved walks recompute the direction at every Euler step, fixed walks reuse the first one. Along c*, t = 1 is a rotation by 1 rad. Along b*, the ideal flow shrinks δ like e−t. With only the group free, the lift is solved from the group's columns of J alone, so its residual says how much of the direction that group can realise.

Systems

Annulus classifier: an MLP (width 32, depth 3, SiLU) separating a disc (r < 0.4) from an annulus (0.6 < r < 1), trained on a 90° wedge or on all angles. Probe: 15 radii × 32 angles.

Mexican hat: the potential network of an ASRNN trained on trajectories of H = ½‖p‖² + ½α‖q‖² + ¼‖q‖⁴. The field is the learned force −∇qV. Here the lift uses 8 radii × 32 angles at the displayed α only (the paper pools rows across α).

Six-fold potential: the same MLP regressing V = r⁴ − 1.2r² + 0.25r⁶cos 6φ, trained on a 120° wedge or on the whole disc. Choose C₂, C₃, C₆ or SO(2) as the target symmetry. Probe: 15 radii × 48 angles, closed under every Cn used.
