A network can fit its training data almost perfectly and still be far from equivariant: where the data do not constrain it, nothing pushes it towards ℰ. Compare each pair: the same architecture and loss, trained on part or all of the symmetric domain. Fit is accuracy for classifiers and relative error for regressions; the spiral's label-smoothed loss cannot go below 0.199. Even with full coverage the spiral keeps a sizeable defect: the labels only fix the sign of the logit, not its size away from the boundary. Click a row to open it.

What you are looking at

Each system is a trained network fθ on the plane. A symmetry group G of rotations acts on the inputs, and on the outputs when they are vectors. The Reynolds projector Pℰ averages f over G, projecting onto the equivariant subspace ℰ: a 64-point quadrature for SO(2), an exact average over the n rotations for Cn. The equivariance defect is δ = f − Pℰf.

The orbit direction ηξ = dΠ(ξ)f, for the rotation generator ξ, rotates the function infinitesimally. It is orthogonal to δ, so moving along it changes where the function points, not how symmetric it is.

The two directions

c* = J+η and b* = J+(−δ). J is the Jacobian of the outputs on a polar probe with respect to the parameters. J+ is truncated at σk ≥ 10−3σ1, and the minimum norm is taken in the sensitivity-normalised gauge (or plain ℓ2, by the switch). The lift residual is the part of the target the parameters cannot reach locally.

Resolved walks recompute J and the direction at every Euler step; Fixed walks reuse the one from t = 0. Along c*, t = 1 is a rotation by 1 rad. Along b*, the ideal flow shrinks δ like e−t. With only the group free, the lift is solved from the group's columns of J alone, so its residual says how much of the direction that group can realise.

The training stage switch loads checkpoints of the same run, at initialisation and after 2% and 10% of training, so you can compare how well the lifts work before and after training. The training loss readout is each network's objective on a sample from its training distribution; it shows what a walk costs the fit.

The coefficient plot shows |c*ᵢ| against |b*ᵢ| for every parameter, in the gauge chosen for the lift. In the normalised gauge the norm minimised is Σ Si² ci², so a parameter with a small sensitivity can carry a large coefficient.

Systems

Annulus classifier: an MLP (width 32, depth 3, SiLU) separating a disc (r < 0.4) from an annulus (0.6 < r < 1), trained on a 90° wedge or on all angles. Probe: 15 radii × 32 angles.

Mexican hat: the potential network of an ASRNN trained on trajectories of H = ½‖p‖² + ½α‖q‖² + ¼‖q‖⁴. The field is the learned force −∇qV. Here the lift uses 8 radii × 32 angles at the displayed α only (the paper pools rows across α).

Spiral classifier: the same MLP separating the two classes of a three-arm spiral, sign sin 3(φ − 2r) for 0.15 ≤ r ≤ 1, trained on a 120° wedge or on the whole disc. The labels have C₃ symmetry only: averaging over C₆ or SO(2) mixes the two classes, so choosing those groups shows what the lift does when the target symmetry is wrong. Probe: 15 radii × 48 angles.

Two-body: the relative coordinate q of two bodies under softened gravity, V = −1/√(‖q‖² + 0.09). The same MLP learns V by matching the force −∇V at positions observed along orbits: three half orbits that all start on the +x axis, or 24 full orbits spread round the circle. Both reach a small training loss, but only the second data set pins down a rotation-invariant potential, so the first converges with a large SO(2) defect. Probe: 15 radii × 32 angles.

Six-fold potential: the same MLP regressing V = r⁴ − 1.2r² + 0.25r⁶cos 6φ, trained on a 120° wedge or on the whole disc. Choose C₂, C₃, C₆ or SO(2) as the target symmetry. Probe: 15 radii × 48 angles, closed under every Cn used.
